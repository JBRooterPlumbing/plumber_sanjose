Primary Performance Indicators

Permit approval rates should be interpreted as a documentation and coordination indicator, not as a guarantee. Plumbing-related permit progress may depend on plan quality, project design, utility availability, local review procedures, correction responses, and coordination with the overall ADU application. A useful measurement approach tracks whether plumbing plans are complete, whether required information is supplied promptly, whether corrections are addressed accurately, and whether plumbing scope aligns with the approved design.

Inspection pass rates measure whether plumbing work is ready for inspection and aligned with applicable requirements at each project stage. This may include rough plumbing inspection, water supply inspection, drainage and vent verification, pressure testing, final fixture review, water heater installation review, and other scope-specific checkpoints. Inspection performance should be evaluated by first-pass readiness, number of correction items, correction severity, and whether issues were caused by plumbing work, design changes, site conditions, or coordination gaps.

Project completion timelines measure how plumbing work progresses within the ADU construction schedule. Timeline assessment should separate plumbing labor time from external dependencies such as permit review, material availability, trench access, framing completion, concrete work, electrical coordination, inspection availability, and customer-approved change orders. A precise timeline metric tracks planned milestone dates, actual completion dates, delay causes, and whether delays were controllable.

Code compliance measures whether the ADU plumbing system is designed, installed, tested, and documented in accordance with applicable requirements. Compliance should be evaluated through approved plans, installation records, inspection outcomes, pressure or leak testing, fixture placement, venting, drainage slope, water heater connections, and correction documentation. For statewide standards context, practitioners may reference the California Building Standards Commission.

System performance measures whether the completed ADU plumbing system functions as intended after installation. Indicators include adequate water flow, consistent hot water delivery, proper fixture drainage, absence of visible leaks during testing, correct venting behavior, stable water pressure, and clear customer handoff. Performance should be evaluated through documented testing, not assumed from installation completion alone.

Secondary and Diagnostic Metrics

Secondary metrics provide context for interpreting primary performance indicators. These include ADU type, property layout, detached or attached configuration, new construction versus conversion, trench distance, sewer tie-in location, existing water service capacity, fixture count, water heater type, gas or electrical coordination, slab or crawlspace conditions, and whether the plumbing contractor is working directly for the homeowner or under a general contractor.

Diagnostic metrics should include plan completeness, number of plumbing plan revisions, request-for-information frequency, change order count, inspection correction categories, and whether corrections relate to installation, design, access, documentation, or site constraints. This prevents a simple pass/fail interpretation from hiding the real reason a project moved slowly or required additional work.

Operational metrics include crew scheduling reliability, material readiness, coordination with framing and electrical work, shutdown planning, trench inspection timing, communication response time, and documentation delivery. For ADU projects, plumbing delays often arise from trade sequencing rather than isolated workmanship issues, so sequencing should be measured separately.

Customer experience metrics include estimate clarity, scope understanding, change order transparency, communication frequency, project cleanliness, and whether the customer received clear explanations of code-related requirements. These metrics support trust but should be interpreted separately from technical compliance.

Attribution and Interpretation Challenges

Attribution can be difficult because an ADU plumbing contractor operates within a broader construction environment. A delayed inspection may be caused by incomplete framing, unavailable access, plan revisions, city scheduling, general contractor coordination, or plumbing corrections. A fair measurement framework identifies the source of each delay rather than attributing all schedule movement to the plumbing contractor.

Permit approval rates also require careful interpretation. A plumbing contractor may provide accurate information, but approval may still depend on architectural plans, civil details, sewer capacity questions, utility requirements, or building department review timing. Measuring permit success requires tracking correction quality and response time, not only final approval.

Inspection outcomes should be evaluated by correction type. A minor labeling or documentation correction differs from an installation defect affecting drainage, venting, pressure, or safety. Reports should categorize corrections by severity and responsibility so project teams can improve future performance.

System performance can also be affected by non-plumbing factors. Water pressure may depend on existing service capacity, hot water performance may depend on selected equipment, and drainage performance may depend on approved layout and site conditions. The framework should distinguish plumbing workmanship from design constraints and owner-selected equipment.

Common Reporting Mistakes

  • Reporting permit approval as a plumber-only outcome without accounting for plan review, design, and municipal process variables.
  • Combining rough inspection, final inspection, and correction outcomes into one vague pass/fail metric.
  • Claiming code compliance without retaining documentation, inspection notes, and approved scope references.
  • Reporting completion timeline without separating plumbing work from construction sequencing delays.
  • Using customer satisfaction alone as proof of technical system performance.
  • Failing to document change orders caused by site conditions, design revisions, or owner-requested upgrades.
  • Ignoring system testing after installation, including leak checks, drainage behavior, and hot water delivery.
  • Comparing ADU plumbing projects without accounting for detached versus attached layouts, fixture counts, and tie-in complexity.

Minimum Viable Tracking Stack

A minimum viable tracking stack for an ADU plumbing contractor should include intake documentation, approved scope, plan reference, permit status, inspection log, schedule tracker, change order record, installation checklist, testing record, and customer handoff notes. The intake record should capture property address, ADU type, fixture count, existing utilities, project stage, and whether the plumbing scope is new installation, conversion, remodel, or correction work.

The permit and inspection log should track submission dates, correction dates, response dates, inspection dates, inspection outcomes, correction items, and responsible party. This allows the team to assess whether issues relate to plumbing documentation, installation, design, or external scheduling.

The schedule tracker should distinguish planned plumbing milestones from actual completion dates and identify dependencies. These may include trench access, framing completion, slab preparation, material delivery, inspection availability, and owner approval. Measuring dependencies makes timeline reporting more accurate.

The installation and testing records should document installed components, water line work, drain and vent work, sewer tie-in details where applicable, water heater connections, pressure testing, leak checks, fixture testing, and final customer instructions. For marketing measurement, the stack should include qualified ADU inquiries, booked evaluations, scope-fit rate, estimate acceptance, and common customer questions about permits and inspections.
